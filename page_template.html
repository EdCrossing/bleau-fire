How severity is measured

NBR  = (NIR − SWIR2) / (NIR + SWIR2)   B08, B12
dNBR = NBRpre − NBRpost
RdNBR = dNBR / √|NBRpre|

Healthy foliage reflects strongly in the near infrared and absorbs in the shortwave infrared, so NBR is high. Fire removes foliage and exposes char and soil, which reverses both, so NBR falls. Differencing isolates that change. RdNBR divides by pre-fire condition, which is meant to correct for how much vegetation there was to lose.

The expected result, and the measured one

dNBR is an absolute difference, so the standard expectation is that it under-reads sparse fuel — less foliage to lose — and that RdNBR narrows the gap between fuel types. Measured here, both fail: heath gives the highest dNBR (0.486) and closed conifer the lowest (0.319), and RdNBR widens the spread rather than narrowing it (0.917 against 0.448).

Why: the index is partly measuring substrate

Calluna heath at Fontainebleau grows directly on quartz sand. After complete combustion the pixel is dominated by bare sand, which has high SWIR2 reflectance. Substituting into the equation, a large ρSWIR2 with modest ρNIR drives NBRpost strongly negative, so dNBR is large regardless of how much energy the fire released. Burnt closed pine at 10 m still contains standing charred trunks and partly green crowns, so its pixel spectrum changes less. RdNBR amplifies the effect because pre-fire heath has a modest NBRpre, shrinking the denominator.

What would separate substrate from severity

Three approaches, in increasing order of cost, none yet run:

  1. Soil-robust burn indices. MIRBI was designed to suppress soil background: MIRBI = 10·ρSWIR2 − 9.8·ρSWIR1 + 2. BAIS2 uses the red edge instead of relying on the NIR/SWIR contrast alone. Both need bands this pipeline does not currently fetch — B11, B8A, B06, B07 — so the first step is widening the band list and re-running.
  2. Spectral mixture analysis. Decompose each pixel into fractions of char, green vegetation, dry vegetation and soil against reference spectra. Severity then becomes the char fraction, which is substrate-independent by construction rather than by correction.
  3. Validation against an independent grading. Copernicus EMS graded this exact fire under EMSR894 by expert interpretation. If that grading also ranks heath highest, the effect is real fire behaviour; if it does not, it is the index. This is the cheapest decisive test and it is the reason the EMS vectors matter.

Until one of those is run, the fuel ranking above should be read as a property of dNBR over this substrate, not as a measurement of fire intensity. Within a single fuel type the ranking remains usable.

Fire weather

The two most extreme days since 1940

What the Fire Weather Index is

The Canadian Forest Fire Weather Index is the system Copernicus EFFIS runs for Europe. It is not a physical fire model — it is empirical moisture bookkeeping, on the principle that how readily fuel burns is mostly determined by how much water it currently holds, and that different fuel layers dry out at very different rates.

It takes four measurements, at noon: air temperature, relative humidity, 10 m wind speed, and rainfall over the preceding 24 hours. From those it maintains three moisture codes, each carried forward from yesterday's value:

FFMC
Fine Fuel Moisture Code — litter and needles in the top 1–2 cm. Responds within hours. Controls whether a fire starts at all.
DMC
Duff Moisture Code — loosely compacted organic matter 5–10 cm down. Time lag about 15 days.
DC
Drought Code — deep, compact organic layers 10–20 cm down. Time lag about 52 days. This is the seasonal drought signal.

Those combine into two intermediate indices and then the headline number:

FFMC + wind → ISI rate of spread · DMC + DC → BUI fuel available · ISI + BUI → FWI
ISI = 0.208 · e0.05039·W · 91.9 · e−0.1386·m · (1 + m5.31/4.93×107)
where W is wind in km/h and m is fine fuel moisture content, derived from FFMC

Why the calculation starts in January, not in July

Because DC has a 52-day time lag, its value on any given day is an accumulation of roughly the previous two months of heat and rainfall. Start the calculation on the day of the fire and you have no history to accumulate, so you must use the standard start-of-season value of DC = 15, meaning saturated ground after snowmelt. The actual value on 12 July 2026 was 486. Running from January rather than July is therefore the difference between a drought term of 15 and one of 486 — the whole seasonal signal. Smoke

What a second satellite saw

Sentinel-5P measures atmospheric absorption rather than surface reflectance, so it is independent of every other number here. Anomalies against a median of 8–10 July.

Two measurements of spread direction disagree

Growth of the burn scar between 13 and 20 July displaced its centroid 1,127 m toward 292°. The plume lies at 264°. Wind over the same window blew toward 211°. So the scar-based estimate is 81° from the wind and the plume-based estimate is within 25°.

The likely cause of the discrepancy is that the two measurements are not of the same thing. Suppression is concentrated at the fire head, which is by definition the downwind edge — 187 water drops were made by four Canadair, two Dash and three helicopter sorties. Downwind growth is therefore the growth most actively prevented, so scar expansion measures fire behaviour net of suppression. Plume transport is unaffected by suppression. This is an explanation, not a demonstration: separating the two properly needs the EMS progression isochrones rather than two satellite passes and a centroid.

Why surface air quality barely moved

Three independent products agree that the fire was detectable from orbit. CO total column reached 0.062 mol/m² against a regional background of 0.029 mol/m², 2 km from the fire. The aerosol layer-height product returned 313 pixels meeting both the quality flag and the smoke threshold on 13 July, and zero on 9 and 14 July — the two control days.

Yet CAMS surface PM2.5 rose only about 1 µg/m³, roughly uniformly across a 60 km ring. Surface concentration for a given emission scales inversely with the depth of the layer the smoke is mixed through:

Csurface ∝ E / (BLH × A)   emission, boundary layer height, area

Boundary layer height on 13 July reached 2,320 m against 1,535 m on the pre-fire baseline — 51% deeper, so about a third lower surface concentration for identical emissions. That is a contributing factor, not a complete explanation: it does not by itself account for a signal this weak, and the remaining candidates are that CAMS did not assimilate this fire's emissions, and that an 11 km model grid cell heavily dilutes a plume of this width.

What the data will not support

The fire footprint is derived, not official

The red outline is mine — gaps in the dNBR ≥ 0.27 mask closed, interior holes filled, components under 200 ha dropped. It exists so the fuel comparison only counts polygons the fire reached. Copernicus EMS published a real delineation under EMSR894; until that is wired in, treat this as a working boundary.

Half the naive burned area is farm harvest

Fields cut between the two dates produce the same signature dNBR detects. Against IGN's RPG register: of 3,711 ha above dNBR 0.10, 1,867 ha sits on farmland. At 0.27 that falls to 118 ha. Forest-only: __FOREST_HA__ ha. This is why the dNBR overlay looks messy over open country — those crisp rectangles are harvest, not fire.

Nothing predicts severity within the fire

Terrain, fuel and access modelled over 60,000 pixels: random 5-fold R² +0.392, spatially blocked R² −0.185. Negative means worse than predicting the mean — no transferable skill at all. The random score was measuring spatial autocorrelation. Together with the weather result, the informative variance for this fire is in the when, not the where within.

Contributed positions, not survey

Boolder's coordinates come from climbers — 10–20 m of error, one to two pixels. Each problem is sampled over a 50 m window and flagged where the plausible range crosses a class boundary. More problems does not mean more precise ones: a circuit-level percentage is far more trustworthy than any single problem's class.
