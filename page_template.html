Reading the passes

Why half the frames are black

The slider steps through every Sentinel-2 pass, not a curated set. Many frames are mostly black with one lit corner. That is not night — Sentinel-2 only images in daylight — and it is not corruption. It is nodata: the satellite's swath clipped the corner of the area and the rest of the frame was never observed.

This matters beyond the animation. Scene cloud cover is computed over valid pixels only, so a granule containing almost nothing advertises almost no cloud — and ranking scenes by cloud actively selects the empty ones. The scene one day before ignition, at 0.6% cloud, is 99.4% nodata here. The label under the slider shows what each pass could actually see.

What was burning

Severity by fuel type

IGN's BD Forêt V2 labels every polygon by vegetation formation and dominant species, so severity can be cut by fuel rather than guessed at. Each pair below is the same ground before and after, sampled from our own imagery at identical brightness — no per-image stretch, or the darkening would be hidden.

__FUEL_CARDS__

This is the opposite of what I predicted. The textbook argument is that dNBR under-reads sparse fuel — less to lose — and that RdNBR compresses the gap. Heath reads highest, closed conifer lowest, and RdNBR widens the spread. Calluna heath here sits on open sand and burns to complete consumption, exposing bright substrate that is highly reflective in shortwave infrared. Over heath the index substantially measures the sand revealed, not the energy released.

Fire weather

The two most extreme days since 1940

Canadian Fire Weather Index from ERA5 hourly data — 759,288 hours, 31,637 daily values, spun up so the long-memory drought codes carry real state rather than startup defaults. The fire ignited on 12 July and made its run on the 13th.

Summer 2026 averaged an FWI of 26.5 against a 1940–2025 mean of 10.2 — rank 87 of 87. Not a bad day inside a normal summer; the most extreme fire-weather summer in the record. A Drought Code of 486 means months of accumulated deficit.

Smoke

What a second satellite saw

Sentinel-5P measures atmospheric absorption rather than surface reflectance, so it is independent of every other number here. Anomalies against a median of 8–10 July.

The burn scar could not confirm the wind: growth after 13 July ran 81° away from it. But suppression targets the downwind head of a fire by definition — aircraft attack exactly where the wind pushes it. Nobody was water-bombing the smoke, so the plume is the honest witness, and the disagreement between them is the argument that the scar-geometry test was measuring firefighting.

Carbon monoxide told the same story from a third product: the column more than doubled the regional background 2 km from the fire. And the height retrieval found 313 confirmed-smoke pixels on 13 July and exactly zero on 9 and 14 July — a clean control. So why did surface air quality barely move? The boundary layer reached 2,320 m on a 35 °C day against 1,535 m before the fire. Smoke stirred through two kilometres of atmosphere is thin by the time it reaches the ground. Dilution, not decoupling.

What the data will not support

The fire footprint is derived, not official

The red outline is mine — gaps in the dNBR ≥ 0.27 mask closed, interior holes filled, components under 200 ha dropped. It exists so the fuel comparison only counts polygons the fire reached. Copernicus EMS published a real delineation under EMSR894; until that is wired in, treat this as a working boundary.

Half the naive burned area is farm harvest

Fields cut between the two dates produce the same signature dNBR detects. Against IGN's RPG register: of 3,711 ha above dNBR 0.10, 1,867 ha sits on farmland. At 0.27 that falls to 118 ha. Forest-only: __FOREST_HA__ ha. This is why the dNBR overlay looks messy over open country — those crisp rectangles are harvest, not fire.

Nothing predicts severity within the fire

Terrain, fuel and access modelled over 60,000 pixels: random 5-fold R² +0.392, spatially blocked R² −0.185. Negative means worse than predicting the mean — no transferable skill at all. The random score was measuring spatial autocorrelation. Together with the weather result, the informative variance for this fire is in the when, not the where within.

Contributed positions, not survey

Boolder's coordinates come from climbers — 10–20 m of error, one to two pixels. Each problem is sampled over a 50 m window and flagged where the plausible range crosses a class boundary. More problems does not mean more precise ones: a circuit-level percentage is far more trustworthy than any single problem's class.
